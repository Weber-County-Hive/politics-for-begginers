Politics for Beginners · How Disclosure Actually Works

The Box Was Empty. Here's How We Still Know That Matters.

Eight Utah officials and candidates, several different offices, one identical box checked: "no conflict of interest to report" — or a form completed with only a fraction of what the public record shows. Here's how independent records surface what a blank form can't hide — and how you can check this yourself, for anyone, anywhere in Utah.

New to this? Start here.

Every elected official in Utah has to fill out a conflict of interest disclosure form — a document listing their jobs, businesses, and board seats, so the public can see where their private interests might overlap with their public decisions.

Here's the catch: the official fills out their own form. There's no one standing over their shoulder checking it against reality before it's filed. So when a line is left blank, the form itself can't tell you whether that's because there was genuinely nothing to report — or because something was left out.

That's not a flaw unique to any one person. It's just how disclosure works everywhere. Which means an empty box is never, on its own, proof of anything. What actually matters is whether something outside that box — a state business registry, a nonprofit's own website, a corporate filing — tells a different story than the form does.

That gap is what this piece is about, using eight real, documented Utah examples.

* marks a claim we genuinely don't know the answer to yet — flagged honestly instead of glossed over
New to this? Two forms get mixed up constantly.

Every case on this page mentions a "conflict of interest disclosure." A few also mention a "campaign finance report." These are not the same document, don't ask the same questions, and don't get filed on the same schedule. Tap each card below.

A candidate can file a spotless campaign finance report while leaving their COI form nearly blank, or the reverse — they're graded separately, filed separately, and a gap in one doesn't fill the gap in the other. Several cases below have both: a clean-looking donor list sitting next to an incomplete conflict-of-interest form.

§20A-11-1604(6) is the common ancestor — it defines what "disclose your interests" actually means, and every other track borrows that same content requirement wholesale rather than writing its own. But which statute actually governs a given form depends on the office: state officials file straight through the Lt. Governor; county officers file under a different section to the county clerk; city officers file under yet another section to the city recorder; and appointed board members — MIDA, PIDs, the Wasatch Open Lands Board — file under Title 67-16, to their own board's clerk. That last one is the self-policing structure this page keeps running into: the body responsible for catching a violation is the body the violator sits on. Campaign finance and lobbyist registration are dotted lines on purpose — different law, different schedule, and a clean one doesn't offset a gap in the other.

How the system is supposed to work

Utah law doesn't leave disclosure to good intentions alone — it's a statutory requirement, with a real penalty attached.

Under Utah Code, officials who qualify as "special public officers" — a category that includes board members of authorities like MIDA, along with many county and municipal officeholders — must file a written conflict-of-interest disclosure once a year, typically by January 31. The statute spells out what happens if they don't:

Utah Code §63H-1-304(5)–(6) — this is MIDA's own law
Title 63H is the statute that created and governs the Military Installation Development Authority specifically. Subsections (5)–(6) apply to MIDA board members — the rule that governs Case 1 (Erik Rowland). A board member who fails to file is guilty of a class B misdemeanor. The administrator or clerk of MIDA's own board is required to report the violation and impose a $100 civil fine — the same body the violating member sits on is responsible for catching and reporting the failure.

The other cases on this page file under different statutes entirely — county candidates and officeholders generally fall under Utah Code Title 67-16 (the Public Officers' and Employees' Ethics Act) or the candidate-disclosure rules in Title 20A-11; USBE members follow the Board's own disclosure requirements. Each has its own version of the same basic ask — list your outside interests — but the specific law, deadline, and enforcement path differs by office. §63H-1-304 is a useful, clearly worded example of how the enforcement side is supposed to work, not the law behind every case here.

The forms themselves generally ask the same handful of questions: current and past employers, any business where you're an officer or owner, board seats — paid or unpaid — and the same information for a spouse. It's a short form. It isn't a hard one to fill out completely.

Former Utah State Auditor John Dougall, June 2026
"Disclose more, not less, and do it publicly... The best practices I saw were when elected officials were just really upfront — 'here's what I do, here's why I have potential conflicts in this arena.' That's when their constituents say, 'OK, they're being upfront, they're disclosing it, they're not hiding anything.'" — Lehi Free Press / American Fork Citizen, June 4, 2026

Dougall, who spent twelve years as state auditor after chairing the Utah House Ethics Committee, put the actual risk plainly: problems rarely come from proven wrongdoing. They come from the appearance of it — from officials who look like they're favoring their own interests without having said so out loud. Disclosure isn't a formality. It's the thing that lets the public tell the difference.

Case 3

Gage Froerer

Weber County Commissioner · in office since 2019, previously Utah House 2007–2019

Froerer's pattern looks different from the other two — not a single blank form, but a documented pattern across years of filings. He also sits on MIDA's board alongside Erik Rowland (Case 1) — both used the same generic county form instead of MIDA's own required disclosure form for that seat. And the developers at the center of his Nordic Village PID vote, John Lewis and Shane Dunleavy, are represented by the same lobbyist as Case 6 below: Kerry Gibson.

His 2025 county disclosure listed Nordic Valley Land Associates, LLC — a company tied to a large Ogden Valley development. His 2026 disclosure, filed one year later, does not list it. In between, Nordic Valley Land Associates was formally dissolved — just five to eight days after his 2026 form was filed.

The same pattern repeats with a second entity: Highlander NP Housing Corporation, listed as a directorship on his earliest House disclosures, is absent from both his 2025 and 2026 county filings — despite remaining a legally active entity through most of the period his 2025 filing covered. It was voluntarily dissolved in February 2025.

Separately — and never appearing on any disclosure form reviewed at all:

  • G & E 2 Properties, LLC — an active two-person real estate entity where he's a named member with signing authority
  • Froerer Appraisal and Investment Company, where he's listed as Vice President
  • C.K.R. Construction, LLC, where he's a listed officer and owner
Employer field — why redact it this year? He's a sitting elected official; the public has a standing interest in knowing where he works. He listed his employer plainly in 2014, 2016, and 2025 — by name, every time. His 2026 form is the first time he's ever invoked the "at-risk government employee" redaction for that field. It's also the same form that drops Nordic Valley Land Associates. The redaction is legally permitted — but after twelve years of freely disclosing the same information, its first appearance lining up with the one entity he also newly dropped is a fair question to ask, not something to wave off as routine.
The gap here is different in kind: not a blanket "no conflicts" answer, but entities that appear on a disclosure form right up until they're formally dissolved — and others that appear to never have been listed at all, even while active. Both patterns are documented from Utah's own corporate registry, not from his own paperwork.
What the entity says it does, versus what its owner does: Wolf Creek Management's 2014 founding document states its purpose as "general management and payroll services to local business." John Lewis's own bios — on his other company's site and on Wolf Creek Resort's own "About Us" page — describe him as a 28-to-32-year real estate developer and general contractor (Lewis Homes Inc.), "a key player in 80% of Ogden Valley's development," and an eight-year former chair of the Ogden Valley Planning Commission. Nothing in his own public description matches payroll or general management services. What Kerry Gibson actually lobbies the state on for this client isn't yet established.

Why it belongs on the form

His filings ask for exactly this: any entity where he's an officer, manager, or owner, at the time he holds it. The form isn't asking him to guess at relevance — it's asking for a complete list, so the public (not him) can judge what matters.

What conflict it could represent

This isn't an unrelated business sitting quietly undisclosed. He voted to create and fund the same public financing structure — the PID and tax-increment diversion — that benefited an entity he had a documented ownership stake in, on a timeline where that stake dissolved around the same votes. That's a direct financial interest in the specific decision, not a background conflict.

Has anyone checked?

Once — but not recently. County attorneys and the State Auditor's office reviewed his Nordic Valley interest in 2022, after a resident's complaint, and found compliance with no sanction. That review covered the original ownership question. It happened before the 2025 filing, before the 2026 filing that drops NVLA five to eight days ahead of its dissolution, and before the first-ever employer-field redaction on that same 2026 form. Nothing since 2022 has been formally reviewed by anyone.

Severe — a documented financial interest in the exact matter voted on; the one formal review is years old and doesn't cover the current pattern

§Conclusion

Eight forms, eight different ways of leaving something out

No two of these look exactly alike — and that's the point worth sitting with.

Rowland and Kahler checked the blanket "no conflict" box. Froerer's forms told the truth right up until an entity was about to dissolve, then went quiet on it. Koecher answered one question out of five confirmed roles. Beesley left an employer field blank entirely while presiding over a vote tied to that same employer. Katrina Gibson listed one restaurant while an active lobbyist license and a same-day land transfer went unmentioned. Bolos's own form didn't arrive with her filing on time. Kerry named two entities correctly while a nonprofit he chairs quietly lobbied the exact committee he presides over. Eight people, eight different shapes of the same gap — which tells you there's no single trick being run here. It's a form nobody's checking, and each person found their own comfortable way to leave things out.

Does anyone actually check these forms?
Functionally, no — but the one time this cycle the system actually enforced something, it wasn't for any of this. In this county's own filing season, the clerk's office forgot to give one candidate his form at all and didn't notice for months. Nobody cross-references a filed form against the state's own corporate registry — the same registry any resident can search for free in under a minute. A form gets collected, filed, and left alone unless someone outside government goes looking.

That contrast is worth stating plainly. A separate 2026 Weber County candidate, Courtney Ryan, was disqualified for arriving 30 seconds late to file — an objection filed by a sitting commissioner with what's been described as unusually specific knowledge of what happened inside the elections office that day, sustained within 48 hours, no cure offered. Meanwhile Ebert's late COI was quietly corrected after a complaint, Bolos's disclosure was quietly moved from the wrong filing bucket to the right one after a text exchange with the clerk, and Katrina Gibson (Case 6) quietly canceled her own active lobbyist license six days after filing — a self-initiated fix rather than a clerk-cured one, but the same shape: correct the conflict quietly, no public notice, rather than disclose it and let voters weigh it. Four candidates, one hard disqualification and three quiet fixes, same election cycle, same clerk's office. None of the disclosure gaps documented across Cases 1-8 triggered so much as a request to amend. The system enforced a 30-second deadline to the letter and let substantive omissions pass without comment. That's not proof of favoritism on its own — but it is proof that "the rules get enforced" and "the rules that get enforced are the ones that matter" are two different claims, and only the first one is true here.

That's why this matters to a vote, specifically. These forms exist to let the public evaluate independence before handing someone a seat that votes on zoning, tax-increment bonds, and contracts worth tens of millions of dollars. Once someone's in office, the conflicts don't disappear — they just start voting. The disclosure form is the one moment that's supposed to let a voter see the whole picture before that starts. When it's incomplete and nobody checks it, that moment quietly disappears, and the only thing standing between the public and an undisclosed conflict is whether someone happened to go look — which is exactly what the tools in the next section let anyone do themselves.

How to check this yourself

This isn't a special skill. Every tool used to find these gaps is free and public.

1. Find the disclosure form. Most counties post current-year filings on their elections or clerk's website. State legislators' forms are archived at le.utah.gov. MIDA and similar authorities are required by statute to post board members' filings on their own website.

2. Search the person's name at the Utah Division of Corporations (secure.utah.gov/bes/). It's free, takes seconds, and shows every business entity where someone is listed as an officer, manager, or member — regardless of whether they mentioned it on their own form.

3. Check the organization's own website. Nonprofits routinely list their boards publicly — "meet the team" pages, annual reports, press releases announcing new members. None of it depends on the official's cooperation.

4. Compare, don't accuse. The finding is the gap between the form and the independent record — not a conclusion about why the gap exists. State what the form asked, what was written, and what the public record shows elsewhere. Let the reader see it.
