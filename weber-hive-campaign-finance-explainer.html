Where the two tracks connect

The same person can sit on both tracks at once: a PAC treasurer can also be a registered lobbyist; a company that donates to a candidate's PAC can also employ the lobbyist working that candidate's committee assignments. Nothing about that overlap is inherently improper — it's legal, and often disclosed. But it means a full picture of "who funds what, and what do they get" usually requires checking multiple, separately-filed records, not just one.

A single contribution, for example, is reported twice: once by the committee sending it, once by the candidate receiving it — on two different schedules, sometimes months apart. Reading only one side shows only half the transaction.

Reference
Key terms
PAC
A Political Action Committee. Collects contributions from members or donors and gives directly to candidates, subject to contribution limits set by state or federal law.
Super PAC
An independent expenditure committee. Can raise and spend unlimited amounts, but legally cannot coordinate directly with a candidate's campaign, and cannot give money straight to a candidate's own account.
PIC
Political Issues Committee. Raises and spends money on ballot measures and issue advocacy rather than candidate elections.
In-kind contribution
A non-cash contribution — goods, services, or paid staff time given to a campaign instead of money. Reported at fair market value, same disclosure rules as cash.
Registered lobbyist
Someone paid to influence legislation on behalf of a client, required to register with the state and disclose which clients and issues they represent.
Amendment
A correction or addition to a campaign finance report already on file. Contributions are often added to an already-"filed" report this way, sometimes weeks or months after the report was first submitted.
Reporting period
A defined window of time — Convention, Primary, a mid-cycle report, General, Year End — each with its own due date, meant to give the public a running look at campaign finances.
Disclosure
The legal requirement to make campaign finance information public. Being technically disclosed and being easy for the public to actually find are not always the same thing.
