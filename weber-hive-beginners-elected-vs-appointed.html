Topic 8

What's the difference between an elected official and an appointed one?

Posted Aug. 28, 2026 · Updated Oct. 17, 2026 · Page look updated Nov. 2, 2026

Short answer

An elected official wins a public vote and answers to voters at the next election. An appointed official is selected — usually by someone who was elected, like a governor — with no public vote at all. Both can hold real government power. Only one of them can be voted out.

When most people think "government official," they picture someone who ran a campaign and won an election — a mayor, a state senator, a governor. That's elected office: your name goes on a ballot, voters choose, and if enough of them are unhappy with you later, they can vote you out.

Appointed office works differently. Instead of a public vote, one person or a small group — often a governor, sometimes a board or commission — simply selects who fills the role. No ballot, no campaign, no direct public say in who gets picked.

Neither path is illegal or unusual — governors appoint advisors, board members, and agency heads all the time, and it's a normal part of how government runs. But the two paths carry a real difference in accountability: if you don't like what an elected official does, you get a chance to vote them out. If you don't like what an appointed official does, there's usually no ballot where you can do anything about it directly.

It's also common for one person to hold both kinds of office over a career — win election to one seat, then later get appointed to a different, unelected role. The power can look similar from the outside. Where it comes from, and who it answers to, is not the same.

Confirmation
Some appointments need a follow-up vote by a legislature or council before they're official — a partial check, but still not a public vote.
Board or commission seat
A common type of appointed role — a governor or agency names someone to sit on a body that oversees a specific area, like housing or transportation.
Senior advisor
A title for a direct appointee to an elected official's office, without formal board oversight — how much authority it carries depends entirely on who appointed them and what they're asked to do.
A real Utah example

Steve Waldrip served two elected terms in the Utah House. In December 2023, Gov. Spencer Cox appointed him Senior Advisor for Housing Strategy — no vote required. A 2026 law then made him state housing coordinator, in charge of an entire new state division. In August 2026, that unelected authority is what he used to tell a city council a county's funding was "at risk."
